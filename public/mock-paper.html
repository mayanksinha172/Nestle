Semaglutide and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Patients
with Type 2 Diabetes — SUSTAIN-6
A Randomised, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Trial

Abstract

Background. The cardiovascular effects of semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, in patients with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk have not been established in a pre-approval outcomes trial.

Methods. We randomly assigned 3,297 patients with type 2 diabetes who were at high cardiovascular risk to receive semaglutide (0.5 mg or 1.0 mg subcutaneously once weekly) or matching placebo. The primary composite outcome was death from cardiovascular causes, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke (MACE-3). The trial was designed to rule out a hazard ratio exceeding 1.8 for the primary outcome.

Results. The primary composite outcome occurred in 6.6% of patients receiving semaglutide versus 8.9% receiving placebo (HR 0.74; 95% CI 0.58–0.95; P=0.02 for noninferiority; P=0.015 for superiority). Rates of new or worsening nephropathy were significantly lower with semaglutide (3.8% vs 6.1%; HR 0.64).

Conclusions. Semaglutide was noninferior to placebo with respect to cardiovascular safety and demonstrated superiority for MACE reduction at 2 years. These findings support the use of semaglutide in patients with type 2 diabetes and high cardiovascular risk.

Introduction

Patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) are at substantially elevated risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), and nonfatal stroke. Regulatory guidance issued by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2008 mandated cardiovascular outcomes trials (CVOTs) for all new glucose-lowering agents.1

Semaglutide is a long-acting GLP-1 receptor agonist with 94% structural homology to native human GLP-1, modified to resist dipeptidyl peptidase-4 degradation and albumin binding to extend its half-life to approximately seven days, enabling once-weekly subcutaneous administration.2 Preclinical data suggested anti-atherosclerotic properties; however, whether these effects translate to cardiovascular risk reduction in a randomised controlled trial setting had not been established.

The SUSTAIN-6 trial was designed primarily to demonstrate cardiovascular noninferiority of semaglutide relative to placebo within the framework of current regulatory requirements. Secondary objectives included assessment of glycaemic efficacy, body weight change, and microvascular outcomes over 104 weeks.

Methods

Trial Design and Oversight. SUSTAIN-6 was a multinational, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial conducted across 230 sites in 56 countries. The protocol was approved by local ethics committees at each participating site, and all patients provided written informed consent. The trial was sponsored by Novo Nordisk, which participated in the design, data collection, and oversight. An independent data monitoring committee provided oversight, and data analyses were confirmed by an independent academic research organisation.

Patients. Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years) with T2D, HbA1c ≥7.0%, and either established cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease (eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73m²), or age ≥60 years with ≥1 cardiovascular risk factor. Key exclusion criteria included recent acute coronary syndrome, planned revascularisation, and end-stage renal disease.

Randomisation and Intervention. Patients were randomised 1:1:1:1 to semaglutide 0.5 mg, semaglutide 1.0 mg, matched placebo 0.5 mg, or matched placebo 1.0 mg, administered via autoinjector pen once weekly. Randomisation was stratified by screening HbA1c (≤8.5% or >8.5%) and insulin use at baseline. Investigators and patients were masked to treatment assignment throughout the 104-week duration.

Outcomes. The primary endpoint was a composite of first occurrence of cardiovascular death, nonfatal MI, or nonfatal stroke (3-point MACE). Secondary endpoints included all-cause mortality, HbA1c change, body weight change, and a composite microvascular outcome comprising new or worsening nephropathy or retinopathy. All events were adjudicated by an independent committee masked to treatment.

Statistical Analysis. The trial was designed to rule out an upper 97.5% confidence interval boundary for the HR exceeding 1.8 (noninferiority margin). The prespecified primary analysis used a Cox proportional hazards model with treatment as a fixed effect and stratification factors as covariates, analysed on an intent-to-treat basis. A hierarchical testing procedure controlled the familywise type I error rate at 5% (two-sided).

Results

Patient Characteristics. A total of 3,297 patients were enrolled (mean age 64.6 years; 60.8% male; mean HbA1c 8.7%; mean BMI 32.8 kg/m²; mean duration of diabetes 13.9 years). Established CVD was present in 83.0% of patients, with 57.6% having a prior MI. The two treatment groups were well-matched at baseline.

Primary Outcome. Over a median follow-up of 2.1 years, the primary MACE composite occurred in 108 of 1,648 patients (6.6%) in the semaglutide group versus 146 of 1,649 patients (8.9%) in the placebo group (HR 0.74; 95% CI 0.58–0.95; P<0.001 for noninferiority; P=0.015 for superiority). Secondary Outcomes. Nonfatal stroke was significantly reduced with semaglutide (1.6% vs 2.7%; HR 0.61; 95% CI 0.38–0.99; P=0.04). Nonfatal MI trended lower (2.9% vs 3.9%; HR 0.74; P=0.12). Cardiovascular death did not differ significantly between groups (2.7% vs 2.8%; HR 0.98). HbA1c was reduced by a mean 1.1 percentage points more with semaglutide, and body weight decreased by 4.4 kg more than placebo at week 104 (both P<0.001).

Microvascular Outcomes. New or worsening nephropathy occurred in 3.8% of semaglutide patients versus 6.1% on placebo (HR 0.64; 95% CI 0.46–0.88; P=0.005), driven primarily by reduction in persistent macroalbuminuria. Retinopathy complications were more frequent with semaglutide (3.0%) than placebo (1.8%; HR 1.76; P=0.02), principally in those with severe pre-existing retinopathy.

Safety. Gastrointestinal adverse events were more common with semaglutide (nausea 21.8% vs 7.7%; vomiting 9.2% vs 2.9%). Serious adverse events occurred at similar rates (35.4% semaglutide vs 35.8% placebo). No unexpected safety signals were identified.

Discussion

SUSTAIN-6 demonstrated that once-weekly subcutaneous semaglutide significantly reduced the risk of MACE compared with placebo in patients with T2D at high cardiovascular risk, thus establishing both cardiovascular safety and superiority beyond the required noninferiority margin.

The 26% relative risk reduction in MACE was driven predominantly by a 39% reduction in nonfatal stroke — the largest such effect reported in a GLP-1 RA CVOT at the time of publication. This finding is consistent with semaglutide's anti-atherosclerotic properties demonstrated in preclinical models, including inhibition of macrophage foam cell formation and reduction in aortic plaque burden.3

The reduction in nephropathy composite was clinically meaningful and preceded the definitive renal outcomes evidence from the dedicated FLOW trial. The retinopathy signal, while observed, was hypothesised to be an artefact of rapid HbA1c reduction in patients with pre-existing advanced retinopathy — a phenomenon previously observed with insulin intensification.4

Conclusions

Semaglutide once weekly significantly reduced the incidence of MACE compared with placebo over 104 weeks in patients with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk, demonstrating both noninferiority and superiority. These results support the cardiovascular benefit of semaglutide and its use in high-risk patients, consistent with subsequent guideline endorsement by the ADA, ESC, and EASD.
